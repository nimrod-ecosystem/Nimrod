Software for screens that anyone can use

Nimrod is free, open-source software that turns an ordinary screen — a laptop, a tablet, a television, a phone that was going in a drawer — into something a person can actually use and control, whatever they are able to move. I built it with the help of AI tools, and I say so here rather than leaving you to wonder.

This page is a proposal. It is the set of sessions I would run at a public library technology centre, with the software running here so you can try it instead of taking my word for it. Everything below works in a browser, on the device you are reading this on, with no account and nothing to install.

What I would teach

Syllabus suggestions

These are sessions I would be ready to run. They are proposals — no dates, no registration, nothing scheduled. Each one says who it is for and how a person can take it — a session that only works for people who can use a mouse is a session that has quietly decided who is coming.

Set up and use a communication board

AAC — augmentative and alternative communication — is any way of saying something when speaking is hard. In ninety minutes we build a board for one real person: the words they actually need, in an order they can learn, big enough for them to hit. Everyone leaves with a board that works on a device they already own, and a printed copy of it for when the battery is flat.

Who it's for: adults and children, and the people around them — family, aides, teachers. No diagnosis, no referral, nothing to qualify for.

How you can take it: by touch, by mouse or trackball, by keyboard, or with one switch that steps through the cards. It runs on a phone, a tablet or a laptop, including old ones. A printed board needs no device at all.

Set up a switch, and make it fit the person

A switch is any button somebody can work reliably — a big round one, a pedal, a pillow switch, a tap on a screen. The hard part is never the switch; it is the timing. How long a press has to be held before it counts. How long to ignore a hand that bounces. How long to wait before it repeats. We set those on real devices, with the person they are for if they can come, and write them down so they can be set up the same way again.

Who it's for: adults, and anybody setting a device up for somebody else. Useful whether the switch is for a child, a parent or yourself.

How you can take it: bring a device if you have one; there will be switches and controllers to try. Everything in the session can be done by touch or with one button.

Games for a classroom or a group

The games here are built so that the thing being scored is the thing being practised — knowing an answer, not saying it clearly or clicking it quickly. In this session we make a set of questions for a real group, play it, and take it away. A teacher, a parent or a group leader leaves with a pack they wrote, running on whatever screen the room has.

Who it's for: teachers, homeschooling families, group leaders, and children and teenagers playing.

How you can take it: four choices and one button, so a switch, a controller, a keyboard or a touch screen all play the same game. Reading aloud is optional.

Run an AI assistant on your own computer

A model that runs on the machine in front of you asks nobody's permission, costs nothing per question, and does not send what you typed anywhere. It is also slower and less capable than the ones in the news, and that trade is the whole subject of the session: what a laptop with no graphics card can actually do, what it cannot, and how to tell which is which.

Who it's for: adults who want to try it without a subscription, and anybody who has been told to be careful what they type into these things.

How you can take it: bring a laptop if you want to install it; the session can be watched without one. Nothing in it needs a paid account.

Status: this is a syllabus, not a finished course. I have written what I would teach; I have not taught it.

An evening for adults

Three shapes that work for a room of adults: a trivia night with question packs the room writes; a painting evening on tablets, where nothing is wasted and nothing has to be cleaned up; and games for couples and pairs, which is mostly an excuse to get two people using one screen without an argument.

Who it's for: adults. No technical background assumed.

How you can take it: any of them can be played by touch, by controller, by keyboard or by switch. Teams work; so does one person and one device.

What I built

The wider project this came out of

This page runs on Nimrod, a free system built to improve daily life for people with serious conditions — and to help the people around them stay close. The main site is at nimrodecosystem.com.

The physical side

A modular connector system for holding a screen, a switch or a tablet where somebody can reach it — parts are 3D-printed, designed in build123d so the source sits beside every part rather than only a finished shape.

The software

The dashboard and modules you just tried — photos, communication boards, games, and the input handling underneath them — are the same code running on this page, not a separate demo built to look like it.
