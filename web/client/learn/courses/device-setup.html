Course C

Connect a Switch: Device Setup & Calibration

What a switch (one big button) is. How it works with a browser with nothing extra to install. And how to tune it to fit one hand, cheek or head movement. You set how long a press must be held, how soon it may repeat, and how to ignore a second press that comes too soon. It is taught with 2 games where timing is easy to see.

Try Wait and Go → Try Comet →
Adults 60 min short · 90 min long Best with a switch or a game controller

What you need

  • A computer with a browser — nothing to install.
  • Best: 1 USB adaptive switch, or a game controller that connects by USB or Bluetooth. The library's own equipment works the same way as a patron's.
  • Without one: a keyboard's space bar is a working substitute for the class.
  • No login needed for the try-it links above.
Short version Longer version Documentation

Short version — about 60 minutes

Plug something in, see it work, tune one number.

  1. 0:00–0:10 — What a "switch" is: anything that sends one signal. It can be a button, a switch pressed with the head, or a tube you sip and puff on, wired to a small circuit board. To a browser, it looks like a key press or a button on a game controller. There is nothing extra to install.
  2. 0:10–0:30 — Plug it in and open Wait and Go: hold off while a charge builds, press when the invite opens. This makes timing visible — how long a press takes, how consistent it is.
  3. 0:30–0:50 — Open Comet and play it with the same switch. Steer toward a heart, then press to catch it. It is a second, different feel for the same switch.
  4. 0:50–1:00 — Talk through what felt hard: too sensitive, too slow to notice a press, tiring to hold. That talk is the real point of the hour.

Longer version — about 90 minutes

Tune a switch to a specific person.

  1. 0:00–0:15 — Recap, then a broader look at input types: switches, gamepads, eye-gaze-to-keypress bridges like Ace Centre's EyeCommander, and plain keyboards.
  2. 0:15–0:45 — Play both games twice. The first time, use the timing as it comes. The second time, first change 2 settings by hand: how long a press must be held, and how soon it may repeat. Compare how the second time feels.
  3. 0:45–1:05 — Two protections and what they are for. One ignores a bounce (a second press that comes too soon). The other lets go of a switch that is stuck down. Also, why a switch that "sometimes presses twice" or "sometimes does nothing" is almost always a timing setting, not a broken switch.
  4. 1:05–1:20 — Group talk. The class describes one person's need. Which of the 2 games felt like the best match for it, and why?
  5. 1:20–1:30 — How this connects to Course B. The same switch works the talk board there, by scanning (the screen steps through the choices one at a time; press when yours is lit). A talk board is also called an AAC board. AAC is augmentative and alternative communication: ways to say something when speaking is hard.

Documentation

What this course is built on, plainly.

One shared place for every control

Every switch, button, dial or controller goes through one shared place in Nimrod. Whatever it is, the browser sees a key press or a button on a game controller. Nimrod turns that into a named action, such as next or select. Any part can use that action. Set it up once. It works the same on every screen.

Wait and Go

A timing game of the kind called go/no-go: you wait, then press at the right moment. A charge builds while you wait. Press when the screen says Go. It writes down every press and release with its timing, including presses the game itself ignored. That is a measurement, not just a score.

Comet

A comet that follows the switch's steering exactly, with hearts to catch by pressing. Movement maps one-to-one to input, so cause and effect are unmistakable — useful both for teaching and for a caregiver watching whether a person can reliably affect something on screen.

An honest limit

Calibration doesn't read the numbers back yet. Wait and Go and Comet both produce real timing data, but nothing in the product turns that data into a suggested hold-time or scan-speed setting automatically — an instructor tunes those settings by hand, watching the same numbers a patron just produced. That's a real gap, not a course simplification, and this class teaches the hand-tuning skill that fills it today.
